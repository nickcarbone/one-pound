Mince on a weeknight

Mince is what much of the world cooks when dinner has to happen on a Tuesday. It browns in minutes, takes seasoning all the way through, and stretches. A pound feeds four when it shares the pan with potato, peas, bread or corn, which is how most of the dishes in this book use it.

Before machines, mincing was knife work.8 The hand-cranked grinder that forces meat through a perforated plate is credited to the German inventor Karl Drais in the nineteenth century, and electric grinders followed as power reached kitchens.8 The older work survives in the words. Picadillo is the little chopped thing,9 and kotlet belongs to the cutlet family, which started out as a rib.15

In the United States ground beef is now close to half of all beef eaten. Derrell Peel of Oklahoma State University put ground beef at 28.6 lb a head in 2025, out of 59.8 lb of beef in all (48%), the highest share since at least 2003.6 The tray itself is regulated: anything sold as ground beef can be at most 30% fat, with no added water, binders or other meats, and the lean-and-fat percentage was put on labels because shoppers could not judge leanness from the name of the cut.7

Every dish here starts from that tray. They come from ten countries, all of them cooked at home there, and seven of them are on the table in 40 minutes or less.

The fat figure is what the pack contains raw, worked out from the label ratio on 454 g. Fat weighs about 13 g a tablespoon, so the second figure is what it would come to melted; a good part of it stays in the meat. Pour fat into a cup to cool and bin it, never down the sink.

Browning a pound

The one technique six of these recipes lean on. Done once here, so each recipe can point back.

  1. Start cold and dry. Take the mince out of the fridge just before you cook. Tip it out of the tray and pat the surface with kitchen paper. Water on the meat is steam in the pan.
  2. Heat the pan first. Put the skillet over medium-high heat for 2 minutes, then add 1 tbsp oil and wait until it shimmers. In the Dutch oven, give it 3 minutes; enamelled cast iron is slow to heat and then holds it.
  3. Lay it in as a slab. Put the pound in as one flat piece and press it 2 cm (¾ in) thick. Leave it alone for 4 minutes. You want a sharp, steady sizzle and a dark brown underside.
  4. Turn and break. Turn it in sections with a spatula and give the second side 2 minutes, then break it up to the size the recipe wants. It is browned when the fat runs clear, the sizzle sharpens again and the edges catch.
  5. Salt at the end. Salt the meat once it has browned. Salt added at the start draws out water and slows the crust.
  6. If water pools. A simmering puddle means the pan is crowded or not hot enough. Keep the heat up and wait: it boils off in two or three minutes, the sizzle returns, and browning starts.
  7. Two pounds. Brown 2 lb in two batches in a standard skillet. Piled in at once it steams grey and takes twice as long.

Browning is for flavour. Doneness is a thermometer reading: 160°F / 71°C for ground beef, pork and lamb, and 165°F / 74°C for ground chicken and turkey.1 Raw mince keeps 1–2 days in the fridge and 3–4 months in the freezer.2

If you don't have it

Chipotle. Leave it out and you have the plain picadillo many families cook. Smoked paprika adds smoke without the sweet-sour adobo, so it moves the dish further than leaving the chile out does.

Half pork. Half ground pork, half beef is a common mix and barely changes the dish; it is a little sweeter and softer.

85/15 or leaner beef. Works, and leaves almost no fat in the pan. Add a second tablespoon of oil so the onion softens properly.

Tinned tomatoes. One 14.5 oz (411 g) tin of diced tomatoes in place of fresh. Out of season it is often better, slightly sharper.

The name comes from picado, chopped. The Real Academia Española gives picadillo as its diminutive and defines it as any dish of finely cut ingredients.9 In Mexico the everyday version is mince simmered with tomato, potato and carrot, finished with peas. It goes onto tostadas, into tacos or beside white rice, and what is left fills chiles or croquetas later in the week.10

This follows the house style Grupo Animal publishes as a recipe for the day-to-day, with one chipotle blended into the tomato for warmth.10 Plenty of cooks leave the chile out altogether, so the mild portion below loses nothing essential.

Checked. No origin story is attached to this dish and none is told here. Grupo Animal uses beef and says pork works too.10

Method

  1. Cut the potatoes and carrots into 1 cm (⅜ in) cubes. Small is what lets them cook in the sauce. Chop the onion finely and mince the garlic. Grate the tomatoes on the coarse side of a box grater into a bowl and throw away the skins. Mince the chipotle and keep it separate with its spoon of sauce.
  2. Brown the beef in the oil in the skillet over medium-high heat, following Browning a pound, about 7 minutes, until the pink has gone and the bits on the base of the pan catch brown. Leave the fat in.
  3. Add the onion and garlic and cook 3 minutes, until the onion is glassy at the edges.
  4. Add the potatoes, carrots, grated tomato, salt and water. Bring to a bubble, then lower to a steady simmer. Cook uncovered for 15–18 minutes, stirring every few minutes and adding a splash of water if the base catches, until a cube of potato crushes easily against the side of the pan.
  5. Stir in the peas and the chipotle with its sauce and cook 3 minutes more. The picadillo should be moist without being soupy: a spoon dragged across the pan leaves a trail that slowly closes.
  6. Taste for salt. Serve on warm tortillas or tostadas, with coriander over the top.

If you don't have it

Sweetcorn. US frozen sweetcorn is bred for sweetness and is wetter than the corn used in Chile. Cook the corn paste a few minutes longer than you think, and skip the sugar on top.

Chicken. Gay's 1862 description includes chicken and many cooks still add some.11 A cup of shredded rotisserie chicken laid over the pino is an easy way to do it.

Olives. Any black olive works. Chilean cooks often leave the stones in, so warn the table if you do.

Blender. This is the one recipe in the book that needs one. A stick blender in a tall jug does the job as well as a jug blender.

A pie without pastry. The filling, pino, is beef cooked down with a great deal of onion and seasoned with paprika. It goes into the dish with hard-boiled egg, black olives and raisins, under a thick layer of sweetcorn ground with basil and cooked with milk, and is baked until the top browns.11

Chile has no exclusive claim to it: versions are eaten in Argentina, Bolivia, Colombia, Ecuador, Paraguay, Peru and Uruguay.11 An early Chilean description appears in Claudio Gay's Historia física y política de Chile of 1862, which describes minced lamb, sometimes with chicken, under ground corn seasoned with sugar, fat and chilli.11 Whether to scatter sugar over the top before it goes in the oven is still argued about in Chile, which is why it is optional here.12

Checked. Origin is shared and contested across South America; the book makes no claim that the dish began in Chile. The 1862 Gay description is cited from Wikipedia's summary of it.11

Method

  1. Put the eggs in the saucepan, cover with cold water, bring to the boil and simmer 9 minutes. Cool them under the cold tap and peel. Heat the oven to 200°C (400°F).
  2. Make the pino. Heat the oil in the skillet over medium heat and cook the chopped onions with a pinch of salt for 10 minutes, until soft and golden at the edges. Push them to one side, add the beef and brown it in the cleared space (Browning a pound), about 7 minutes. Stir in the garlic, paprika, cumin, oregano, 1 tsp salt, the pepper and 60 ml (¼ cup) water. Simmer 5 minutes, until moist but not wet. Taste: the pino needs to be well seasoned, because the corn is mild.
  3. Blend the thawed corn with the basil and milk until thick and mostly smooth; some texture is fine. Rinse the saucepan, melt the butter in it and add the corn with ½ tsp salt. Cook over medium heat, stirring constantly with a long spoon, for 8–10 minutes, until it thickens to the texture of soft polenta and pulls away from the base. It spits, so keep stirring.
  4. Spread the pino over the base of a 20 cm (8 in) square baking dish. Tuck the quartered eggs, the olives and the raisins into it. Spread the corn over the top right to the edges and smooth it. Scatter the sugar over now if you are using it.
  5. Bake 25–30 minutes, until the edges bubble and the top has set. Then put it under the broiler (UK grill) for 2–4 minutes, watching it, until the top browns in patches. Rest 10 minutes before cutting.

If you don't have it

Marjoram. Dried oregano is its closest relative and the nearest swap. It is sharper and less sweet, so use a little less.

Half beef. Pork or beef are both standard.13 Half and half gives a firmer, beefier patty.

Breadcrumbs. Use plain fine crumbs. Panko makes a spikier, crunchier crust that is less like the Polish one.

Dill. Flat-leaf parsley, on the potatoes and in the salad. The salad without dill is still good, but plainer.

Culture.pl calls the minced cutlet one of Poland's popular, everyday meat dishes. It is a fixture of milk bars and lunch counters, and it became especially common under communism, though it is older than that: nineteenth-century cookbooks give recipes for minced beef, pork or veal cutlets, and the first Polish cookbook, from the seventeenth century, has a related meatball of chopped meats.13

At home it is a Sunday standard. The mixture is pork, a roll soaked in milk, egg, fried onion and marjoram, coated in breadcrumbs and fried, and the plate usually has boiled potatoes and a cucumber salad beside it.14 The word kotlet belongs to the cutlet family, from the French côtelette.15

Checked. The communist-era popularity and the older cookbook history come from Culture.pl.13 No single origin is claimed.

Method

This recipe works the mince raw into a mixture, so it skips Browning a pound.

  1. Peel and halve the potatoes and put them in the saucepan with cold salted water. Bring to the boil and cook 15–20 minutes, until a knife slides in without resistance. Start them first.
  2. Tear the bread into a bowl, pour over the milk and leave it 5 minutes to go soft.
  3. Chop the onion finely and cook it in 1 tbsp of the butter in the skillet over medium heat for 5 minutes, until soft and pale gold. Tip it onto a plate to cool. Keep the skillet: you will fry in it.
  4. Squeeze the bread lightly so it is wet but not dripping. Add it to the pork with the egg, the cooled onion, marjoram, salt and pepper, and work it with one hand for 1–2 minutes, until the mixture binds and feels slightly sticky.
  5. With wet hands, shape 8 ovals about 2 cm (¾ in) thick. Press each into the breadcrumbs on both sides.
  6. Heat the oil and the rest of the butter in the skillet over medium heat until a crumb dropped in sizzles at once. Fry in two batches, 4–5 minutes a side, until deep golden and firm when pressed. If the crust darkens before 4 minutes, turn the heat down: the inside needs the time.
  7. While they fry, make the salad: slice the cucumber very thin, salt it and leave it 5 minutes, then squeeze out the water and stir in the sour cream and chopped dill. Drain the potatoes and toss them with a knob of butter and more dill.

If you don't have it

Bread. Egyptian baladi bread is thinner than US pitta. Pitta works but stays softer at the centre; split very thick pockets and use the thinner halves.

Leaner beef. Go no leaner than 85/15. The fat is what crisps the bread from inside, and lean hawawshi is dry.

Tahini. Already on the shelf from Ten Weeknights. Plain yoghurt with garlic and lemon is a fine sauce instead; pickles are the other usual side.

Minced meat, onion, peppers and spices spread thin inside bread and baked until the bread crisps and the fat soaks through it. It is sold across Egypt as street food, and Egyptian recipe sites publish oven versions for cooking at home.1617

The usual origin story names a Cairo butcher, but even his name is uncertain (Mohamed El-Hawawshi or Ahmed El-Hawawsh), and the date is given as the 1930s by some and the 1970s by many. The earlier date rests on a photograph of a receipt said to come from his shop, which nobody has verified.16 Treat the story as unconfirmed.

Checked. Origin contested: the butcher's name and the decade are both disputed and the evidence is weak.16 Egyptian versions often mix beef and lamb; this one is all beef.

Method

This recipe works the mince raw into a mixture, so it skips Browning a pound.

  1. Heat the oven to 220°C (425°F) with a rack in the middle. Line the sheet tray with foil or baking paper.
  2. Grate the onion and squeeze it hard in your hands to get rid of the liquid. Finely chop the pepper, the chilli, the tomato (seeds out) and the parsley, and mince 2 garlic cloves.
  3. Mix the beef with the onion, pepper, tomato, parsley, garlic, tomato paste, cumin, coriander, salt and pepper with your hand until even. If you are keeping a mild portion, fill two pittas now (see below), then work in the chilli and chilli flakes.
  4. Cut a slit along the top third of each pitta to open the pocket. Spread a sixth of the filling inside, in a thin even layer about 1 cm (⅜ in) thick, right to the edges, and press flat. Thin is what lets the meat cook through before the bread burns.
  5. Brush the outsides with the melted butter and lay them on the tray. Bake 10 minutes, turn them over, and bake 8–10 minutes more, until the bread is crisp and brown and fat is bubbling at the edges. For extra crunch, 1 minute under the broiler (UK grill) at the end.
  6. Meanwhile whisk the tahini with the juice of the lemon, the last garlic clove grated, a pinch of salt and cold water a spoonful at a time. It seizes, then loosens into a pourable sauce. Cut the hawawshi into halves and serve with the sauce.

If you don't have it

Chutney. The Stellenbosch recipe uses peach or mango chutney as well as apricot jam.19 If you keep mango chutney, use 1 tbsp of it and 1 tbsp of jam and drop the lemon; it tastes rounder and spicier. Jam and lemon gets the sweet-sour balance without a single-use jar.

Mince cooked with curry powder, a little fruit and milk-soaked bread, then baked under a thin layer of egg and milk. It is associated with the Cape Malay community and is now often called South Africa's national dish. It is served with yellow rice.1819

Its history is disputed. The word may come from the Malay boemboe, curry spices, or from bobotok, an Indonesian dish with quite different ingredients; Wikipedia calls the etymology contentious.18 Stellenbosch University's account says food researchers trace the recipe to Dutch traders moving between Indonesia and the Cape in the seventeenth century, while other writers point to European layered meat dishes.1918 None of these is settled, and the book does not choose between them.

Method

  1. Heat the oven to 180°C (350°F). Tear the bread into a bowl and soak it in 60 ml (¼ cup) of the milk.
  2. Heat the oil in the skillet over medium heat and cook the chopped onion for 6–7 minutes, until soft and golden. Add the garlic, curry powder and ½ tsp of the turmeric and cook 1 minute, until fragrant.
  3. Turn the heat to medium-high, add the beef and brown it, breaking it up (Browning a pound), about 8 minutes. If more than a tablespoon or so of fat has pooled, spoon it off.
  4. Squeeze the bread, keeping the milk, and stir it in with the jam, lemon juice, raisins, 1 tsp salt and the pepper. Cook 3 minutes and taste: it should be savoury first, then gently sweet and sour.

Method

This recipe works the mince raw into a mixture, so it skips Browning a pound.

Where the clock won

Every place this book shortens or simplifies the dish as it is cooked at home.

  • Picadillo Mexico — The potato and carrot cook in the sauce. Grupo Animal boils them separately first and adds tomato purée; here the tomatoes are grated straight in.10
  • Pastel de choclo Chile — Frozen US sweetcorn stands in for fresh Chilean corn, and the pie is baked in one dish rather than the individual clay dishes some households use.
  • Hawawshi Egypt — US pitta stands in for Egyptian baladi bread and bakes a little softer.
  • Bobotie South Africa — Apricot jam and lemon replace the chutney-and-jam mix, and the bobotie bakes in the skillet the filling was cooked in.
  • Kotlet Iran — The potato is grated raw instead of boiled and cooled first, which saves about half an hour.
  • Qeema matar Pakistan — Paprika and cayenne replace South Asian red chilli powder, and the covered simmer is shorter than Tea for Turmeric's.
  • Clear-braised lion's head China — Traditional lion's head uses hand-chopped pork with about half fat.24 Supermarket mince is finer and leaner, so the meatballs are denser. Jicama stands in for water chestnut.
  • Xíu mại Vietnam — VnExpress steams the meatballs before they go into the sauce.25 Here they cook raw in the sauce, which saves the steamer and a pan. The prawns are optional.

Kotlety mielone, Tori soboro don are cooked the way they are at home.
